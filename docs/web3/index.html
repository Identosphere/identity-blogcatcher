Cypherpunks, Seated and Sponsored

Nobody hid the agenda. It was printed on the invitation.

The ticket cost $200, and you could only pay in ZEC.

Wednesday, October 14. National Union Building, Washington, D.C., sixth floor. Reception at five, a seated dinner with keynotes at six-thirty, then a fireside chat. It sold out. One attendee’s recap called it an incredible night of networking, and I have no reason to doubt that.

The name on the invitation was the Cypherpunk Policy Dinner.

What follows looks at the word on that invitation: what it’s being asked to mean now, and how little effort anyone made to hide the shift.

Nothing was concealed

This was not a covert operation. The organizers, a group called Pretty Good Policy for Zcash with programming from Project Glitch, say exactly what they were doing on the event page. They invite cypherpunks to meet Washington, and they pitch the evening as cypherpunk values getting a seat at the policy table. In their own words: “The juxtaposition is the point.”

Fine. Take them at their word. That’s the interesting part. Nobody is smuggling an agenda into the room. The agenda is the dinner.

What the word Cypherpunk used to mean

In 1993, Eric Hughes wrote the document that gave the movement its creed. Privacy in an open society, he argued, won’t be handed over by governments or corporations, because they have no structural reason to grant it. You build it yourself. In code. Then you defend it yourself.

To be fair to everyone involved, cypherpunks have never avoided policy. The Crypto Wars of the 1990s were policy fights, over export controls and the Clipper Chip, and the people who fought them spent years making their case to Washington. The event page points to one of its keynote speakers having worked at Coin Center on defending the freedom to publish code. The DC Privacy Summit the next day lists Cindy Cohn among its speakers, the attorney who led the Bernstein case, the First Amendment challenge to the government’s crypto export rules. That’s a real thread in the movement’s history, and nobody should pretend otherwise.

So the question isn’t whether cypherpunks should ever speak to policymakers. They always did. The question is what they ask for when they get there.

Asking for the right to write and use privacy software without permission is one thing. Asking for a seat at the table where the rules about it are made, over dinner with the people who make them, is another. Both can be called policy engagement. Only one of them fits what Hughes actually wrote.

Who was in the room

Here is the lineup for the dinner, taken from the organizers’ own pages.

One keynote came from Josh Swihart, CEO of ZODL and the former chief executive of the Electric Coin Company. The other came from Landon Zinda, a Senior Advisor on the SEC’s Crypto Task Force and Counsel to the Chairman. The fireside chat was moderated by Julie Stitzel, Chief Policy Officer at Digital Currency Group, one of the firms that led ECC’s 2016 funding rounds.

The dinner’s own page names one sponsor: the Zcash community itself, through a grant from Zcash Community Grants. The dinner is billed as an associated event of the DC Privacy Summit, held the next day in the same building, and the Summit’s sponsor page is a longer list. DCG is its premiere sponsor. Its event sponsors include Circle, the law firm Brown Rudnick, TRM Labs, and Cypherpunk Technologies, the Nasdaq-listed company readers of an earlier piece here (“The Cypherpunks Who Aren’t”) will remember. The dinner page also carries the standard language government attendees need for their ethics reviews, the kind of paperwork that makes a dinner a recognized Washington event.

That last detail isn’t a scandal. It’s just how this city works, and the organizers followed the form. But it tells you what kind of evening this was: a Washington event with a cypherpunk name, rather than a cypherpunk event that happened to be in Washington.

The sponsor that sticks in the throat

One name on that list needs explaining.

TRM Labs is a blockchain intelligence company. Its own site describes the business plainly: it sells tools that trace funds across blockchains, to law enforcement agencies, national security agencies, regulators, tax authorities, prosecutors, and banks. Its materials say hundreds of law enforcement agencies use its platform, and that its products let frontline investigators check whether a crypto address has been tied to illicit activity, from a phone, on the spot.

If that sounds familiar, it should. It’s the same industry earlier pieces in this series described: the one that grew up to read public ledgers and attach names to them. Chainalysis is the best known. TRM is its direct competitor.

So here is a fact, stated without adjectives. The summit that the Cypherpunk Policy Dinner is billed as part of lists among its sponsors a company that sells cryptocurrency tracing to law enforcement.

Now the fairness, because it’s owed. To be exact, TRM sponsors the Summit, and the dinner’s own page doesn’t list it. Sponsoring an event doesn’t mean writing its agenda, and nothing here shows TRM did. Every company with a stake in crypto policy shows up in Washington, and TRM may well argue that its work and financial privacy can coexist. Maybe so. What TRM did there is unknown to me and beside the point. What matters is that it was a natural place for it to be.

Picture the 1993 mailing list reading that guest list and sponsor page. A tracing vendor among the sponsors, a venture investor’s policy chief at the microphone, a regulator’s senior staff at the keynote, a law firm, a public company named after the movement. Then ask which of them the manifesto told you not to wait on.

The legibility tax, paid in person

None of this is irrational. If you hold an asset that wants an ETF wrapper, institutional lending, and mainstream listings, you need to be legible to the institutions that grant those things. Legibility means meeting the people who decide. Meeting them means dinners, and dinners cost money, and sponsors sponsor. It’s the logic of any industry that wants acceptance.

It’s also the logic this series keeps returning to. Reach in this space is allocated by intermediaries, and intermediaries extend it to projects they can talk to. Somebody has to be at the table to be talked to. Somebody has to pay for the table. The dinner is that cost, itemized.

Reasonable people can decide the price is worth paying. Calling it cypherpunk is where the argument starts.

There’s an old instinct in politics: the surest way to blunt a challenger is to invite it in. Sociologists call it co-optation, absorbing the people who might oppose an institution into its own structures until the opposition has a seat, a title, and a dinner. Nobody has to plan it. A word that once meant refusing to ask permission can end up on a ticker symbol, a conference badge, and a policy dinner’s invitation, and each step feels like progress. By the end, the word is still there. The edge isn’t.

What it looks like when nobody can host the dinner

PIVX has no natural host for this event. There is no company to organize it, no corporate sponsor to write the check, no policy officer on staff. The community could vote to fund one, and nothing in the project’s structure rewards it for doing so. No one speaks for the project in Washington.

That has a real cost, and this series has never pretended otherwise. A project nobody can represent is a project nobody amplifies. The reach problem hasn’t gone away (“Freedom of Speech, Not Freedom of Reach”).

So apply the manifesto’s own test. Hughes gave cypherpunks one job: write code. Build the systems, defend your own privacy with them, and don’t wait for any institution to grant it. Hold both sides of this story up against that.

Zcash gave this field its best cryptography, and that credit stands. Around the cryptography now sits a layer of structure: venture funding in its history, a Nasdaq-listed holding company wearing the movement’s name, an ETF, and a Washington dinner with a venture investor’s policy chief at the microphone, attached to a summit with a blockchain tracing vendor among its sponsors. Each of those is a legitimate business choice. None of them is code.

PIVX’s side of the ledger is short. A fair launch. No venture round, ever. No company, no board, no stock market ticker, no ETF. A treasury that pays for work only after a public vote. And a good part of what that treasury pays for is the building itself: zkSNARKs shielded transactions on a proof-of-stake since 2021, a user-friendly wallet that runs in your browser, a private messenger on Nostr that’s still in beta and being improved, and shielded staking, on the roadmap for the 6.x line with a v6 testnet already running.

In early 2021, PIVX shipped Zcash’s Sapling zk-SNARK protocol, customized to run on proof-of-stake, before Ethereum moved to proof-of-stake itself. Not bad for a micro-cap, self-financing, community-supported DAO.

Shielded staking would let holders stake shielded coins and collect the rewards directly to a shielded address. That matters beyond convenience. Only a small share of the supply sits in the shielded pool today, and a thin pool is easier to watch: when little else is moving, an observer can match a deposit to a later withdrawal by amount and timing. That technique is called timing analysis. As more holders stake this way, rewards flowing into the pool add steady shielded activity and draw more coins in, which gives every shielded transaction more cover.

Nobody building any of this is waiting for a regulator’s staff to find them credible. They ship, and a community of people who bleed purple keeps funding it, vote after vote, with no jackpot at the end. A masternode operator doesn’t need a dinner to remember why they locked 10,000 PIV.

None of this makes anyone at that dinner a villain. Cypherpunk is no trademark, plenty of people in that room write good code and mean what they say, and I’ve said so before. But the test was always what you do and what you depend on, never what you call yourself. A project that depends on venture backers, a public company’s balance sheet, and a regulator’s goodwill is exposed to pressure from all three. PIVX’s code and its funding depend on none of them. Its market access is a different story, as the Binance delisting showed, and that is the cost named above.

That’s the whole difference. Nobody can hand PIVX a seat at the table, so nobody can take it away. No regulator’s dinner can become a condition of its existence. The code runs, the masternodes vote, the treasury funds the work, and none of it waits for anyone’s approval, in any city.

Nobody lied about what this was. They printed it on the invitation. Put it next to the 1993 text, then look at who has spent ten years doing the one thing that text asked for.

Your most private questions, and how AI chatbots have exposed them

It’s 11 p.m. Your lab results have just landed in your patient portal, and one number is flagged in red. Your doctor’s office won’t open until Monday, so you ask the AI assistant beside the page what it means. Across town, someone has a debt collector’s letter open in a tab and asks the AI to explain their rights. Someone else, with a divorce settlement on screen, asks what they’re about to sign. A manager asks for a summary of the confidential layoff plan in her inbox, while one of her employees, reading the company’s HR policy, asks whether he has a case against her. Every one of them assumes the conversation, and the page beside it, will stay private.

These are the conversations people now have with AI, and together with the pages they share, they reveal far more than a search query ever could. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman has said people use ChatGPT “as a therapist, a life coach”. In a 2026 KFF poll, a third of U.S. adults said they’d turned to AI chatbots for health information, and 41% of them had uploaded personal medical information such as test results or doctors’ notes. OpenAI itself reports that more than a million ChatGPT users a week have conversations showing signs of suicidal planning. And at work, a 2025 LayerX report found that 77% of employees who use AI tools paste data into them, with more than a fifth of those pastes containing personal or payment-card information.

With most AI services, every one of those conversations, and every page shared along with them, sits on someone else’s servers, readable by the AI company and the other companies behind it. Once they’re there, five things can go wrong:

Used to train the next model. A 2025 Stanford study found that six leading U.S. AI developers appear to train their models on users’ chats by default, and some keep those chats indefinitely. Handed over to a court. In the New York Times’ copyright lawsuit, a federal judge ordered OpenAI to preserve users’ ChatGPT conversations, including deleted ones, and then, in November 2025, to hand over 20 million de-identified chat logs. OpenAI said it knew of no court ever ordering “wholesale production of personal information at this scale”. Sam Altman had warned months earlier that if there’s a lawsuit or order demanding data, “we could be required to produce that.” Leaked through bugs and open databases. In March 2023, a ChatGPT bug showed some users the titles of other people’s conversations. In January 2026, the popular Chat & Ask AI app left its database open, exposing about 300 million messages from more than 25 million users, including some of their most sensitive questions. Published for anyone to find. In 2025, more than 370,000 Grok conversations became searchable on Google after shared links were indexed without users’ knowledge, and thousands of shared ChatGPT conversations turned up in search results before OpenAI pulled the feature. Meta AI’s Discover feed filled with medical and legal questions from people who didn’t realize they had posted them publicly. Even among conversations people shared on purpose, a 2026 study of 100,342 of ChatGPT, Copilot, and Gemini conversations found sensitive personal data in about 8%. Answered by a different model. Providers can quietly substitute another model for the one you selected, and a NeurIPS 2025 study found such swaps can’t be reliably detected from the answers alone.

End-to-end encrypted Brave AI is built so that none of this can happen to your conversations. Brave and NEAR AI never hold a readable copy of your chats, so there’s nothing readable to train on, hand over, or leak. Your history stays on your device, the model’s maker never sees your words, and attestation shows which model is answering you. Openness, transparency, and verifiable privacy: Brave principles older than AI, now built into Brave AI

In 1883, cryptographer Auguste Kerckhoffs argued that security shouldn’t depend on keeping a system’s design secret: a good system stays safe even when everyone can see how it works. A century later, Unix co-creator Ken Thompson, in his 1983 Turing Award lecture “Reflections on Trusting Trust” showed that public source code can still deceive you: a compromised compiler can insert a backdoor that appears in no source code. His conclusion was stark: “You can’t trust code that you did not totally create yourself.” Since no one writes all their own code, the lesson we draw is that openness matters, but it isn’t enough. You also need to verify what actually runs.

This thinking shaped Brave long before today’s AI boom. In 2014, Brendan Eich, who went on to co-found Brave with Brian R. Bondy, co-authored Trust but Verify, warning that browser vendors could be compelled to secretly ship surveillance code. Citing Thompson’s attack, it called for independent verification: researchers rebuilding software from public source code and checking that it matched what users actually received. Brave carries that forward: our browser is developed in public, and its code is open source.

AI raises the stakes. As the previous section showed, people share their most personal information with AI assistants with little visibility into who can read it, how it’s processed, or even which model is responding. Too often, privacy comes down to a provider saying “trust us.” That’s privacy perfume: reassuring claims sprayed over systems no one can check.

At Brave, we believe privacy should be something you can verify, and Brave AI puts these principles into practice:

Openness: the protocol, the relay, the verifier, the model’s weights, and NEAR AI’s code running inside the TEEs are all public. As Kerckhoffs would have it, the only secrets are the keys, and the one that unlocks your conversation, the TEE’s private key, never leaves the TEE. Transparency: this post and the code behind it show exactly where your data goes and who can see what, and attestation confirms that your answers come from the model you chose, not a cheaper substitute. Verifiable privacy: attestation proves which code is actually running inside the TEE, and our verification oracle rebuilds NEAR AI’s code from public source to confirm it matches. That’s a practical step toward solving Thompson’s problem. Only then does your browser get the key it needs to lock your conversation end to end, so that only the TEE can unlock it. And openness doesn’t mean compromising on quality: in our latest head-to-head evaluation, from June 2026, Ask Brave, powered by open-weight Qwen3 and Brave’s LLM Context API, outscored every AI answer engine we tested, including Grok, Google AI Mode, ChatGPT, and Perplexity.

And because the weights are public, the model the TEE attests to is one anyone can download, inspect, and benchmark. Closed models can run in TEEs too, but only under their makers’ control, with weights no one else can examine.

They show what a model is, not how it was made, and they can’t rule out malicious changes to the weights: researchers have shown that backdoors can be planted in a model’s weights in ways that are, in some cases, provably infeasible to detect. That’s why openness has to extend to training. Nvidia now releases its Nemotron models with much of their training data and their training recipe, not just their weights. But open training matters only if you can verify it, and today it has a reproducibility problem: a published recipe still asks you to trust that it’s the one actually used. Verifiable open training closes that gap. Gensyn’s open-1b makes every step of its training reproducible and checkable.

Brave Research is moving in this direction too: our Confidential-DPproof shows that training can be verified even when the data must stay private. And even a fully verified model doesn’t make AI private on its own: whoever runs it could still read your conversations. That’s why Brave AI pairs open weights with open code and verifiable hardware, so you depend far less on trusting the model’s maker, the company running it, or us. Together, they bring us closer to something AI has long been missing: privacy you don’t have to take on trust.

Step 1: The TEE proves its identity before the browser receives its key

Before your browser can encrypt anything, it needs the TEE’s public key, and that key is only handed out after the TEE proves what it is.

First, our verification oracle audits every NEAR AI TEE deployment before Brave approves it:

Genuine hardware. It verifies Intel’s signature on the TEE’s hardware attestation (a TDX quote), proving the CPU enclave is real Intel TDX hardware. The oracle then rebuilds the images from that source and confirms the result matches what’s running. That’s Thompson’s lesson in practice: we verify not just the source, but what actually runs. Before Brave approves any update to NEAR’s code, the change is reviewed, including an automated privacy check that it doesn’t introduce any new way for your prompts to leak. The model you chose. It confirms the attested model is the one requested, and uses a hardware-attested deployment log to prove exactly which model image is serving. The third-party model runtime isn’t rebuilt from source, so it is pinned by that log instead. Keys tied to the enclave. It records a signing key only if that key is bound into a verified hardware attestation.

Every check must pass. Only then does the oracle record its approval of each software image and signing key.

Then the open-source Brave AI Gateway fetches the TEE’s live attestation for the model you selected and checks it against those approvals. The TEE’s encryption key must carry a valid digital signature from an approved signing key. Only a key that passes every check is sent to your browser, and the gateway re-checks before relaying each message, using a verification no more than an hour old. If verification fails, Brave detects the change and won’t hand out the key or relay your message.

Today, your browser relies on the gateway for this check. Next, your browser will check each key against a signed list of the oracle’s approvals itself. After that, it will verify the hardware attestation directly, so you no longer need to trust any Brave server to vouch for the TEE.

Step 2: Sealed in your browser with Oblivious HTTP

With a verified key in hand, your browser seals the message using Oblivious HTTP (OHTTP), an IETF standard, through Chromium’s built-in OHTTP support. Under the hood, OHTTP uses Hybrid Public Key Encryption (HPKE, RFC 9180):

For each request, your browser generates a fresh, one-time (ephemeral) X25519 keypair. It runs an elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman (ECDH) key exchange between that ephemeral key and the TEE’s public key, producing a shared secret. HKDF-SHA256 turns the shared secret into a symmetric key. The entire chat request, including your conversation, is encrypted with AES-128-GCM, an authenticated cipher that also detects tampering. Your browser sends the ephemeral public key and the ciphertext. Only the TEE, which holds the matching private key, can recompute the shared secret and decrypt.

Replies come back sealed too. The TEE encrypts its answer, streamed piece by piece, under a key derived from the same exchange, so only your browser can read it.

Each turn is a new request: your browser generates a fresh one-time key and re-encrypts the entire conversation history.

Step 3: Your IP address, separated from what you ask

Encryption hides what you say, but whoever receives a request can still see where it came from. OHTTP’s answer is to split the job between two parties, a relay and a gateway, so that neither sees both your IP address and what you said.

Despite its name, the Brave AI Gateway plays OHTTP’s relay role, and the gateway that decrypts your message sits inside NEAR AI’s TEE. OHTTP privacy guarantees have been formally analysed.

Your browser sends the sealed message to the Brave AI Gateway, which acts as the relay. Brave sees your IP address but can’t read the message, because it’s encrypted to the TEE. Brave drops your IP and forwards the ciphertext to the NEAR AI TEE (the gateway) under Brave’s own account, so to NEAR AI every request looks like it comes from Brave. The TEE decrypts and answers the message but never learns your IP address.

What a Couples Therapist Would Tell This DAO

Conflict isn’t necessarily aproof that something’s broken.

If the PIVX community sat down in a couples therapist’s office, the therapist would say something that sounds almost rude at first: of course it’s like this.

A community isn’t a hand-picked team. It’s whoever shows up, from a species that produces generous people and cruel ones, calm people and reactive ones, the occasional genuinely well-adjusted adult and the occasional person running on trauma they’ve never once looked at directly. A DAO doesn’t filter any of that out. It just hands all of it a voting mechanism and a shared treasury and says, good luck. Well, at least the ones that own masternodes. But everyone has a voice.

So the question was never “why does our community have conflict.” Conflict was guaranteed the moment more than a handful of real humans showed up and started caring about the outcome. The actual question is whether it knows how to fight well. Most don’t. That’s not a PIVX problem. That’s a humans problem wearing PIVX merch.

The paradox built into the model itself

A good number of people in the PIVXcommunity have read Rick Falkvinge’s Swarmwise, free online, often treated as close to scripture for how a leaderless movement is supposed to work. It’s a genuinely useful book. It’s also carrying a contradiction most of its readers never clock, because Falkvinge states it plainly, almost in passing, and then builds a model that runs directly against it.

The line is this: accountability and authority go hand in hand.

Then the rest of the book describes a structure built specifically to avoid granting anyone formal authority. Leadership in a swarm isn’t appointed, it’s earned purely by inspiring people, moment to moment, with nothing backing it up but whether others feel like following right now. No one can tell anyone else what to do. That’s the whole design, and it’s a real strength, it’s a big part of why swarm-style organizing can move fast and resist capture.

But read those two ideas side by side and the problem announces itself. If accountability requires authority, and the model deliberately has none, then holding anyone to anything was always going to be fragile by design. The person trying to enforce a line the community already agreed to has no backing, no formal process, no higher body to appeal to. Just whatever social force they can generate in the moment. When that’s not enough, and it often isn’t, they’re left standing there alone, holding a rule nobody’s required to respect.

Structurelessness doesn’t remove power. It just stops you from seeing it

This isn’t a new problem, and it isn’t a crypto problem. In 1970, Jo Freeman wrote an essay called “The Tyranny of Structurelessness” about the feminist collectives of her era, and it’s been cited by basically every leaderless movement since because it names something uncomfortable and true: refusing to build formal structure doesn’t eliminate power. It just makes the power that’s already there, confidence, charisma, tenure, social capital, informal and impossible to check. A group that insists it has no hierarchy still has one. It’s just unacknowledged, which means it can never be held accountable the way an acknowledged one could be.

A DAO with no formal authority isn’t a DAO without power dynamics. It’s a DAO where the power dynamics are real and deniable at the same time, which is worse for the person trying to hold a line, not better. They’re not just fighting the rule-breaker. They’re fighting the community’s own insistence that no one here actually has power to begin with.

When the person enforcing the rule becomes the villain of the story

There’s a specific, well-documented pattern worth naming directly, because once you see it, you’ll recognize it instantly: DARVO. Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender. Psychologist Jennifer Freyd documented it originally in contexts far more serious than DAO governance, but the mechanism is identical wherever it shows up. Someone breaks an agreed-upon line. Called on it, they don’t address the violation. They deny it happened, attack whoever raised it, and recast themselves as the one under attack.

Watch for it and you’ll see the shape every time: the person holding the community to its own stated rules gets rebranded, in real time, as the aggressor. Not because the facts support that. Because it’s an extremely effective move, and because a structureless group has no mechanism to stop the reframing from taking hold once enough people are confused about who actually started it.

This isn’t abstract for PIVX specifically, and it’s worth being concrete about why. Since I joined this community in 2019, PIVX has stood out against the broader crypto space, loud by default, frequently hostile, often unmistakably hypermasculine, as something genuinely friendlier, as purple. That’s not an accident anyone just enjoyed. It got written down, deliberately, as actual guidelines on the community’s Discord, agreed to by a clear majority, aimed squarely at not scaring off newcomers before they ever got to the technology. A privacy coin for everyone has to actually include people who don’t already know how to survive crypto Discord’s default tone, or the “for everyone” part is just a slogan.

Those guidelines get challenged reliably, and the challenge almost always arrives dressed as a free speech objection. It’s worth being precise about why that doesn’t hold up, rather than just asserting it. Free speech, as a principle, protects your right to say something. It has never meant a right to say it inside someone else’s community, free of any agreed consequence, and it has certainly never included the right to insult. Asking people to be respectful isn’t restricting anyone’s speech. It’s declining to hand a platform to a specific kind of speech nobody was ever owed a platform for in the first place.

One careful distinction matters here, because it’s easy to blur. Disagreeing, in good faith, about exactly where a guideline should sit, whether a specific rule is too strict, too vague, enforced unevenly, is healthy, normal, and should be welcomed outright. That’s not DARVO. DARVO isn’t “I think this line is drawn wrong.” It’s break the agreed line, get called on it, and respond by denying, attacking, and recasting the person enforcing the community’s own stated agreement as the actual aggressor. Those are two entirely different things, and treating every objection as the second one would be its own kind of bad faith.

But here’s the part that actually matters, once that distinction is clear: DARVO shows up constantly, in politics, in families, in every kind of institution that has ever existed. Its sheer prevalence everywhere else is not an argument for tolerating it here. A community that spends real, continuous, deliberate effort trying to stay friendly, open, and welcoming doesn’t get to wave through a widespread bad pattern just because it’s widespread. If anything, the opposite is true. The fact that something is common everywhere else is exactly why a place that worked hard not to be like everywhere else shouldn’t quietly let it back in through the side door.

Reading the book isn’t the same as living it under pressure

Here’s the part that explains why some of the people most fluent in the language of healthy process still fail at it completely the moment things get tense. There’s a real difference, well documented in therapy, between declarative knowledge and embodied behavior. Being able to describe a healthy boundary calmly in a forum post is a different skill than holding that boundary when you’re actually angry, embarrassed, or scared of losing status. Plenty of people can recite the theory perfectly and then abandon every bit of it the second it costs them something personally.

This isn’t hypocrisy, exactly, or at least not only that. Intellectualizing, understanding a concept so thoroughly that the understanding itself starts to feel like the work, is a recognized defense mechanism. It lets a person feel like they’ve done something by knowing the right answer, without ever doing the much harder thing of actually behaving differently under stress.

The reality everyone builds for themselves

And underneath all of it sits the plainest, most universal mechanism of all: motivated reasoning. People don’t usually experience themselves as the one breaking the rule. They’ve already, often without noticing, reasoned their way to a version of events where they were right, a version where the rule didn’t really apply to them, a version where the other person started it. This isn’t a character flaw unique to difficult people. It’s one of the most replicated findings in psychology, and it applies to every single person in this conversation, including whoever’s reading this and nodding along about someone else.

Now, the part worth actually reading twice

None of the above is a reason to feel worse about this community. If anything, it’s close to the opposite.

Here’s what a good therapist tells a couple who shows up exhausted, convinced their fighting means something’s fundamentally broken: conflict was never the warning sign. Avoidance is. The research on this is about as clear as social science gets, relationships that last aren’t the ones with no conflict, they’re the ones that repair well after it. Gottman’s decades of research on couples found that the presence of disagreement predicts almost nothing about whether a relationship survives. What predicts it is whether the people in it can come back together afterward, honestly, without pretending nothing happened and without staying wounded forever.

A community where nobody ever fought about anything wouldn’t be healthy. It would be a community where nobody cared enough to bother. The fights happening in PIVX’s community aren’t evidence the project is failing. They’re the noise made by a genuine number of people who actually give a damn about the outcome, which is a far rarer and more valuable thing than it sounds like from inside the exhaustion of watching it happen.

And if you’re one of the people this exhausts, the one who runs a node quietly, who shows up and votes thoughtfully on proposals, who welcomes a confused newcomer patiently for the fourth time this month, who has never once wanted to be in the middle of a public fight about who said what to whom, here’s the thing worth hearing directly: you are not failing at this because you hate the conflict. You are not required to be the one who wins the argument, outlasts the aggressor, or fixes the people who came here carrying their childhood trauma this community never signed up to treat. That was never your job.

Your job, the one that’s actually been building this network for a decade, is the unglamorous, undramatic, compounding work that was never going to trend on social media. It’s the thing the conflicts are, underneath all the noise, actually fighting over the right to continue doing. The loud moments are visible precisely because conflict is loud by nature, not because they’re the majority of what’s actually happening here. Most of what sustains this project every single day is quiet, and it’s being done, right now, by people exactly like you, whether or not anyone ever tells you that directly.

So: let the people who want to fight about governance fight about governance. Keep doing the thing you were already doing. Ten years from now, nobody will remember the argument. The node you kept running, the wallet feature you tested, the person you helped get set up, that’s the part that was never going to show up in anyone’s screenshot, and it’s also the only part that was ever actually load-bearing.

Sound Money Needs More Than Scarcity

Bitcoin passes most of the classical tests for money. The one it fails isn’t small.

Bitcoin’s case for being sound money rests on a real, serious argument, one worth taking at full strength before questioning any part of it. Economists have used a standard set of properties to evaluate candidates for money for a very long time: durability, portability, divisibility, verifiability, scarcity, and fungibility. Bitcoin’s advocates didn’t invent this framework to flatter their asset. It predates Bitcoin by centuries. It’s also the right framework to actually use here, because it lets both Bitcoin and PIVX be measured against the same bar, rather than one built to favor either side.

Run Bitcoin through it honestly, and it earns high marks on almost everything.

Where Bitcoin genuinely delivers

Durability: a Bitcoin can’t rot, corrode, or degrade, its existence is guaranteed by a distributed network rather than a physical object that can be damaged or lost to time. Portability: value that once required an armored truck to move now moves at the speed of a broadcast transaction, across any border, to anyone, without asking permission. Divisibility: a single bitcoin splits into 100 million satoshis, fine enough for any transaction size that matters. Verifiability: anyone can audit the entire supply and every transaction in it, no trust required, no institution’s word to take.

And scarcity, the property Bitcoin is most famous for, is real and mathematically enforced. 21 million coins, full stop, a supply schedule no committee can vote to change. That’s a genuinely hard thing to build, and it’s why Bitcoin earned the nickname “digital gold.” The comparison is apt in more ways than one, which becomes relevant in a moment.

Where it fails, and fails structurally

Now the sixth property: fungibility. This is where the classical case for Bitcoin as money runs into a problem that isn’t small, isn’t temporary, and isn’t fixable without changing what Bitcoin fundamentally is.

Fungibility means every unit is interchangeable with every other unit, no bitcoin should be worth more or less, or more or less usable, than any other bitcoin, based on where it’s been. That’s not a nice-to-have for money. It’s close to the whole point. Money exists so strangers can transact without needing to investigate each other’s history first. The moment a currency’s units start carrying baggage, that function breaks down.

Bitcoin’s entire transaction history sits permanently on a public ledger, visible to anyone, forever. In practice, this doesn’t stay theoretical. Chain-analysis firms exist specifically to trace that history, and exchanges and custodians routinely act on what they find, flagging, discounting, or outright refusing coins whose history touches a mixer, a sanctioned address, or anything else their algorithms don’t like. That means, in the real world, not every bitcoin is treated the same. Some are worth less, or worth nothing at certain venues, purely because of where they’ve been, through no action of the current holder. That’s a textbook fungibility failure, and it’s structural, not a bug that gets patched. It’s the direct, unavoidable consequence of a permanent public ledger, the same design choice that gives Bitcoin its verifiability.

Give Bitcoin its due for the trade-off it made. Public verifiability is genuinely valuable, and Bitcoin chose it deliberately. But a currency can’t fully deliver both total transparency and full fungibility at the same time. That’s not a criticism, it’s a fact about what each design choice costs. Bitcoin picked transparency. The bill for that choice comes due exactly where fungibility is supposed to live.

What changed since 2009

It’s worth being precise about why this wasn’t always the obvious criticism it is today.

In 1993, Eric Hughes’ Cypherpunk Manifesto laid out a goal that Bitcoin, launched in 2009, did more than anything before it to actually deliver on: money that didn’t require permission from, or visibility to, any institution. And for Bitcoin’s first several years, that promise held up reasonably well in practice, not because the ledger wasn’t public, it always was, but because almost nobody had built the tools needed to actually exploit that transparency at scale. Chainalysis, the company that essentially invented commercial blockchain forensics, wasn’t founded until 2014, five years after Bitcoin launched, and it was born directly out of investigating the Mt. Gox collapse, not out of some pre-existing surveillance infrastructure waiting for Bitcoin to arrive.

That gap has closed entirely. Blockchain forensics is now a multi-billion-dollar industry, Chainalysis alone has raised over $500 million and is valued at $8.6 billion, serving governments, exchanges, and banks across dozens of countries. KYC requirements are close to universal at any on-ramp or off-ramp a normal person would actually use. And the tooling keeps compounding: Chainalysis’s own current marketing explicitly promotes AI systems built to scale investigation and compliance work to, in the company’s own words, the speed of crypto. What took a determined analyst days to trace by hand a decade ago, an automated system now does continuously, at scale, across the entire chain, indefinitely.

None of that required Bitcoin to change. The ledger works exactly the way it always did. What changed is everything built to read it. A design that delivered on the manifesto’s promise reasonably well in 2009, simply by virtue of nobody having built the surveillance layer yet, doesn’t deliver on that same promise by default in an environment where deanonymizing a public ledger is now a mature, well-funded, AI-assisted industry. The values haven’t moved. The technology required to actually protect them has, and Bitcoin’s architecture was fixed years before that technology arrived.

The other classical question: does anyone spend it?

There’s a second, quieter problem worth taking seriously, and it’s one mainstream monetary economists were making arguments about long before cryptocurrency existed, not a crypto-native complaint invented to attack Bitcoin specifically.

An asset that’s widely expected to keep appreciating faster than the price of goods and services gives its holders a straightforward, rational incentive: hold it, don’t spend it. Every purchase becomes an opportunity cost against a rising asset, and the smart move, individually, is always to wait. That’s a fine strategy for a savings vehicle. It’s a real problem for something trying to also function as a medium of exchange, the thing that actually circulates and gets used, rather than sitting untouched in cold storage. This is precisely why most functioning currencies, historically and today, target mild, predictable, positive inflation rather than a hard cap or deflationary schedule, not because inflation is virtuous in itself, but because a currency people are incentivized to hoard is failing at half of what money is supposed to do.

PIVX’s supply isn’t fixed. It follows a disclosed, algorithmic tail emission, a fixed, flat number of new PIV created every block, indefinitely, rather than a hard cap or a halving schedule that eventually approaches zero. The absolute emission doesn’t shrink. What does shrink, steadily, is the resulting inflation rate as a percentage of total supply, since the same flat number of new coins matters less every year against an ever-larger base. That’s a deliberate design difference, not an oversight, and measured against the classical goal of money that actually circulates, it points the right direction. A currency whose supply grows slowly and predictably gives holders less reason to sit on it indefinitely, and more reason to treat it as something meant to move.

Where this actually lands, and it isn’t “sell your Bitcoin”

None of the above is an argument that Bitcoin is a bad place to store wealth. Its scarcity case is real, its track record is real, and plenty of people have entirely legitimate reasons to hold it for exactly that purpose. I’m not arguing that PIVX should replace Bitcoin as a store of value. That would be a much weaker, much less interesting point, and it isn’t the one worth making.

The actual argument is narrower and, I think, considerably stronger: Bitcoin cannot fix its own fungibility problem without abandoning the transparent ledger that makes it Bitcoin. That gap doesn’t need to stay open, though, because nothing requires that the same asset handle both jobs, storing value and moving privately through the world, by itself.

Think of it as two different moments in money’s life, not two competing coins fighting for the same role. Bitcoin can do what it does best: sit as a scarce, durable, verifiable store of value, digital gold, held for years, unspent. PIVX exists for the other moment, when value actually needs to move, when it stops being savings and becomes a purchase, a payment, a transaction with another human being who doesn’t need or deserve a window into everything that wallet has ever touched. PIVX’s zk-SNARK shielded transactions break the public trail at exactly that junction. Not by hiding a crime. By restoring the property money is supposed to have by default, and that Bitcoin’s own design permanently forecloses on: no history attached to the unit changing hands.

The one honest caveat worth stating plainly: getting from Bitcoin to PIVX still requires an exchange somewhere, and if that exchange is the kind that collects your identity, a link could exist at that single point. This is worth taking seriously rather than waving away. It’s also a solved problem in practice, not a theoretical gap. PIVX’s own website lists a range of exchanges that require no personal information at all, swap services built specifically around no registration, no email, and no KYC, several explicitly designed for exactly this kind of private conversion. Once PIVX is in a shielded wallet, spending it doesn’t require going back through a centralized, identity-linked venue at all, there are already real ways to convert PIV directly into everyday purchasing power, gift cards, goods, and services, without an account or a KYC form anywhere in the chain. The mechanics exist today. The gap between “sound store of value” and “actually private to spend” isn’t hypothetical, and it isn’t unsolved.

The reframe

This article was never really about Bitcoin versus PIVX, and treating it that way undersells the actual point. Bitcoin built something genuinely remarkable: scarce, verifiable, portable digital value, the store-of-value half of sound money, done about as well as it can be done on a fully public ledger. What it can’t do, by its own founding design choice, is fix its fungibility on its own. That’s not a failure of effort or imagination. It’s the direct cost of the transparency Bitcoin chose on purpose.

PIVX isn’t trying to win the store-of-value argument. It’s built for the part of sound money that Bitcoin’s own architecture permanently can’t provide for itself, privacy at the moment value actually moves, from savings into the world, between one person and another, the way money was always supposed to work before every transaction came with a permanent, public paper trail attached.

What is a zAccount

A Panther zAccount is a private, onchain identifier that acts as a secure gateway to confidential DeFi transactions. Through the zAccount, users can satisfy compliance requirements, hold zAssets—Panther's privacy-enhanced representations of deposited assets—and participate in confidential transactions within Panther: 

Creation & Activation: Users connect their externally-owned account (EOA), complete compliance requirements through approved third-party providers, and activate their zAccount. These requirements may include identity verification, wallet risk screening and other eligibility checks. A unique zAccount ID is registered in a public lookup table maintained by Panther’s smart contracts. Root Keys: Each zAccount is tied to two root public keys—one for spending and one for reading. These enable secure access to zAssets while keeping operations shielded. UTXO Integration: zAccounts are implemented as special UTXOs (Unspent Transaction Outputs), which represent individual spendable records, in append-only Merkle trees. Every zAsset UTXO is assigned to a specific zAccount owner via unique spending keypairs. This ensures only the rightful owner can spend assets, while non-interactive transfers allow seamless, private peer-to-peer movement.

This architecture delivers:

Unlinkability: External observers can’t easily connect your trades back to your Externally Owned Account (EOA). Non-Interactive Transfers: Send zAssets privately to another zAccount holder using their public read/spending keys from the registry. How zAccounts interact with Panther’s MASP

zAccounts strike a powerful balance between privacy and real-world usability, allowing privacy and compliance to coexist within the same infrastructure. Panther’s zAccounts interact with the MASP when users swap assets, mint them within the shielded pool, and withdraw them. 

Deposit & Mint: Link your EOA, activate your zAccount, and deposit assets to mint zAssets inside the shielded pool. Private Trading: Use zSwap via DeFi adaptors—all tied to your zAccount and bundlers. External observers can not directly link the swap to the user´s identity or EOA. MEV Protection & Alpha Safeguarding: Trading strategies, position sizes, and timing remain hidden, reducing the risk of front-running and strategy replication. Withdrawal: Redeem zAssets back to your EOA or another address, with full user control. Rewards & Fees: Certain activities within Panther may earn Panther Reward Points (PRPs), a non-transferable internal rewards mechanism that encourages participation and helps strengthen the protocol's privacy set. Users can also manage gas and protocol fees directly from their zAccount balances. Panther's zSwap lets users execute confidential, privacy-preserving token swaps on DEXs where users can choose bundlers, regular or fast processing, and max slippage.

Zone Managers define entry rules, but once inside, users enjoy strong anonymity sets shared across the pool—making individual transactions harder to distinguish. Unlike fully anonymous mixers (which face regulatory hurdles) or transparent DEXs, zAccounts enable programmable privacy—strong defaults with flexible compliance hooks.

On 10 September, Global Digital Finance (GDF) convened two roundtables with the Bank of England, with participation from the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), GDF members and industry experts. Both sessions were held under the Chatham House Rule.

The first roundtable focused on the Bank of England’s draft Code of Practice for systemic stablecoins and the proposed joint Bank of England-FCA regulatory approach. Moving beyond the headline policy choices, participants tested how the regime would work in practice across real business models, payment flows and market infrastructure. Key themes included how the temporary £40bn issuance guardrail would be reviewed and adjusted, what T+0 redemption means once KYC/AML checks and cross-border payment rails are factored in, the treatment of custody and omnibus structures, and the value of a phased transition into systemic supervision. A wider theme was that the UK regime must work across the full stablecoin ecosystem, not only at issuer level.

The second roundtable used GDF’s Global Stablecoin Regulatory Playbook as a framework for discussing international regulatory convergence, functional equivalence and cross-border recognition. With broad agreement across major jurisdictions on what a credible fiat-backed stablecoin regime should achieve, the discussion centred on how different national regimes can interact. Participants explored the distinction between redemption and convertibility, the Bank’s openness to deference for overseas-issued systemic stablecoins, the risks of liquidity fragmentation from multi-issuance requirements, and the role of international standard setters such as the FSB and FATF.

The central takeaway was that the next phase of stablecoin regulation is less about making rules identical and more about building the regulatory trust, evidence and supervisory mechanisms that allow well-regulated stablecoins to operate across borders without unnecessary fragmentation.

This report draws on a GFTN/Ripple Policy Summit roundtable at Point Zero Forum, building on GDF’s Global Stablecoin Regulatory Playbook, to examine the gap between well-understood stablecoin regulatory principles and their inconsistent, jurisdiction-bound implementation.

It covers:

(1) the need for a shared taxonomy and classification of stablecoins by risk profile rather than label.

(2) the core building blocks of credible domestic frameworks — reserve composition, redemption rights, and proportionate financial-stability requirements.

(3) two competing models for scaling stablecoins across borders — multi-issuance (unified token, multiple local issuers) versus equivalence/recognition regimes (home-jurisdiction oversight extended abroad) — with examples from the EU, UK, Singapore, and UAE.

(4) three near-term priorities for closing the gap: FSB-aligned gap analyses, presumptive equivalence built on existing bilateral cooperation, and supervisory colleges that assess issuer networks collectively. The report concludes that the regulatory tools already exist and what’s needed now is deliberate adoption, jurisdiction by jurisdiction.
